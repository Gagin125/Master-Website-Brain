# Influence - Extracted Principles

Source: Robert B. Cialdini, *Influence*

Folder: Core Behavioral Psychology & Dopamine

Extraction status: usable PDF text extraction.

Purpose of this note: extract ethical persuasion principles for websites, not pressure tactics.

---

## Core Thesis

People often rely on mental shortcuts when deciding what to trust, choose, or avoid. The major influence principles are:

- Reciprocation
- Liking
- Social proof
- Authority
- Scarcity
- Commitment and consistency
- Unity

For websites:

> Trust is built by giving visitors real reasons to believe, not by visually shouting or forcing persuasion tricks.

---

## Website-Relevant Ideas

### 1. Reciprocation

People feel warmer toward brands that provide useful value before asking for action.

Website translation:

- Give visitors helpful information before demanding contact.
- Show prices, location, process, menu, service details, FAQs, or examples.
- Make the site useful even before conversion.

Good use:

- A restaurant shows menu, location, hours, reservation link.
- A service provider offers a clear checklist or audit.
- A product page explains care, sizing, delivery, and return details.

Bad use:

- Fake "free" gifts that are only lead traps.
- Pushy popups that ask for email before value is delivered.

---

### 2. Liking

People are more open to businesses that feel human, familiar, warm, and aligned with their taste.

Website translation:

- Real photos matter.
- Tone of voice matters.
- Founder/team presence can help when trust is personal.
- Design should make the brand feel like someone real is behind it.

Good use:

- Real team and location images.
- Natural, unforced copy.
- A visual identity that matches the brand's personality.

Bad use:

- Generic stock photos.
- Over-polished AI imagery.
- Fake warmth or fake founder stories.

---

### 3. Social Proof

When uncertain, people look at what others have done or said.

Website translation:

- Reviews, testimonials, case studies, client logos, usage numbers, press, and real customer photos reduce uncertainty.
- Social proof is strongest when it is specific and relevant.

Good use:

- Real Google reviews.
- Testimonials with names, context, and specific praise.
- Case studies that show before/after or measurable outcome.
- "Trusted by" sections only when true.

Bad use:

- Fake testimonials.
- Anonymous praise without context.
- Inflated numbers.
- Review screenshots without respecting platform/customer rights.

---

### 4. Authority

People trust credible expertise, credentials, awards, experience, and professional signals.

Website translation:

- Authority should be shown through evidence, not self-flattery.
- Certifications, years of experience, press, awards, safety standards, awards, official partnerships, and expert explanations can all help.

Good use:

- "Award-winning restaurant in Vilnius" if verifiable.
- Professional photography and well-maintained details.
- Clear business information and contact routes.

Bad use:

- Vague "industry-leading" claims.
- Fake badges.
- Decorative authority symbols with no backing.

---

### 5. Scarcity

People value limited things more, but scarcity is easily abused.

Website translation:

Scarcity can be legitimate when it is real:

- limited tables
- seasonal dishes
- limited event capacity
- appointment availability
- handmade stock limits

Good use:

- "Reservations recommended for weekend evenings."
- "Seasonal menu changes monthly."
- "Private event dates are limited."

Bad use:

- Fake countdown timers.
- False "only 2 left" claims.
- Artificial urgency that damages trust.

---

### 6. Commitment And Consistency

Small commitments can make larger actions easier, especially when they feel aligned with self-image.

Website translation:

- Let users take small low-friction steps.
- Make the next step feel consistent with what they already want.

Examples:

- View menu before reserving.
- See examples before booking a call.
- Choose a service path before submitting a form.
- Save directions before visiting.

Bad use:

- Multi-step funnels that trap users.
- Making cancellation or exit difficult.

---

### 7. Unity

People respond strongly to shared identity: "people like us", community, membership, tribe, local belonging.

Website translation:

- Local businesses can use place, neighborhood, culture, language, and shared rituals.
- But unity must be real, not costume.

Good use:

- Local language done properly.
- Real neighborhood context.
- Authentic community photos/events.
- Brand voice that fits the audience.

Bad use:

- Pretending to belong to a community without proof.
- Exploiting identity anxiety.

---

## Practical Website Rules Extracted

- Give useful information before asking for action.
- Use real human proof wherever possible.
- Make authority specific and verifiable.
- Use scarcity only when true.
- Treat social proof as trust support, not decoration.
- Build warmth through real assets and tone.
- Let small actions lead naturally to larger actions.
- Use shared identity carefully and honestly.

---

## Trust Signals For Websites

Strong trust signals:

- real reviews
- real business address
- working phone/email
- real photography
- transparent prices or starting points
- social profiles
- active Google Business Profile
- clear process
- clear policies
- awards/certifications if real

Weak trust signals:

- generic stock people
- vague claims
- fake urgency
- low-quality logos
- empty social proof
- hidden contact details

---

## Ethical Filter

Before using an influence principle, ask:

- Is the claim true?
- Would the business be comfortable explaining this tactic openly?
- Does it help the user decide, or does it pressure the user?
- Is the proof specific and verifiable?
- Does it respect user autonomy?

If the answer is no, avoid it.

---

## AI Agent Instructions

When designing a website:

1. Identify which trust barrier matters most.
2. Choose proof that directly reduces that barrier.
3. Use influence principles as support, not tricks.
4. Never invent scarcity, reviews, authority, or community.
5. Place social proof near the decisions it supports.
6. Keep persuasion calm and brand-appropriate.

## Candidate Rules For Active Brain Later

- Persuasion should make decisions easier, not less free.
- Social proof works best when it is real, specific, and close to the CTA.
- Authority must be evidenced, not merely claimed.
- Scarcity should only be used when genuinely true.
- Real human warmth beats fake brand friendliness.
- Local identity is powerful when it is authentic.
