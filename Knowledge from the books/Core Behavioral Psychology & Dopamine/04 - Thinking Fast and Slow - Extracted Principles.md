# Thinking, Fast and Slow - Extracted Principles

Source: Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

Folder: Core Behavioral Psychology & Dopamine

Extraction status: usable PDF text extraction.

Purpose of this note: extract design-relevant decision psychology without overstating what a website can control.

---

## Core Thesis

Human judgment is shaped by two modes of thinking:

- System 1: fast, automatic, intuitive, associative, effortless
- System 2: slower, deliberate, analytical, effortful

For websites:

> Visitors first feel and scan before they deeply reason. Design must support fast understanding, then provide enough evidence for slower evaluation.

---

## Website-Relevant Ideas

### 1. System 1 Sees Before System 2 Reads

People form quick impressions from visual signals before carefully reading.

Website translation:

- Hero sections matter because they shape first trust.
- Typography, spacing, imagery, contrast, and polish affect perceived quality instantly.
- A confusing first viewport creates friction before copy has a chance.

Good use:

- Clear first impression.
- Strong visual hierarchy.
- Real images.
- Obvious brand/product/place signal.

Bad use:

- Vague hero headlines.
- Generic visuals.
- Cluttered first screens.
- Design that makes the visitor work too hard.

---

### 2. Cognitive Ease

People prefer things that are easy to process. Fluency can create a feeling of truth, safety, and confidence.

Website translation:

- Clear layouts feel more trustworthy.
- Familiar navigation patterns reduce friction.
- Good contrast, spacing, and readable type reduce mental effort.
- Scannable pages help visitors understand quickly.

Important restraint:

- Cognitive ease is not the same as oversimplification.
- Premium pages can be sophisticated, but they should not be confusing.

---

### 3. Substitution

When a hard question is difficult, people often answer an easier one without noticing.

Website examples:

- "Is this business competent?" becomes "Does this site look professional?"
- "Will this restaurant be good?" becomes "Do the photos look appetizing and real?"
- "Can I trust this service?" becomes "Do they show proof and sound clear?"

Design implication:

- Surface-level signals matter because they become shortcuts for deeper judgments.
- This is why cheap design damages trust even when the actual business is good.

Ethical warning:

- Do not use polish to hide weak substance.
- Use design to accurately communicate quality.

---

### 4. Availability

People judge likelihood or importance by what comes easily to mind.

Website translation:

- Recent reviews, memorable images, strong examples, and visible proof make quality easier to recall.
- If a site does not show proof, visitors may rely on doubts, stereotypes, or competitors.

Good use:

- Put the most persuasive proof close to key decisions.
- Use concrete examples.
- Show the actual experience/product.

Bad use:

- Hiding proof too low.
- Relying only on generic claims.

---

### 5. Anchoring

Initial information influences later judgment.

Website translation:

- The first section anchors perceived brand quality.
- The first price anchors price expectations.
- The first photo anchors perceived atmosphere/product quality.

Practical implication:

- Do not waste the hero.
- Do not open with weak assets.
- Do not put low-value details before core value.

---

### 6. Framing

People respond differently depending on how the same information is framed.

Website translation:

- "Cheap website" vs "cost-effective starter website" changes perception.
- "Book a table" vs "Reserve your evening" changes mood.
- "Contact us" vs "Plan your visit" changes intent.

Good use:

- Frame actions around user value and context.
- Keep the frame honest.

Bad use:

- Misleading positive framing that hides real tradeoffs.

---

### 7. Loss Aversion

People often feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains.

Website translation:

Loss framing can work, but it is risky.

Acceptable use:

- "Avoid waiting by reserving ahead."
- "Do not miss seasonal dishes this month" if seasonal limitation is true.
- "Keep your SEO value with redirects" in technical guidance.

Risky use:

- Fear-heavy copy.
- Shame-based conversion.
- Fake scarcity.

---

### 8. Peak-End Rule

People remember experiences partly by intense moments and endings.

Website translation:

- Small moments of delight can make a site feel cared for.
- The final CTA/form confirmation/handover matters.
- Microinteractions can create polished peaks.
- The footer/contact/reservation experience should not feel neglected.

Good use:

- Smooth language switch.
- Clear success states.
- Refined hover states.
- Pleasant confirmation messages.

Bad use:

- Flashy animation that distracts from purpose.
- Broken final forms.
- Footer that feels like an afterthought.

---

## Practical Website Rules Extracted

- Design for fast scanning first, then deeper reading.
- Reduce cognitive load wherever the visitor needs to decide.
- Use visual hierarchy to prevent competing signals.
- Put proof where doubt appears.
- Treat the hero as the first anchor.
- Use framing to clarify value, not manipulate.
- Make final interactions feel calm and polished.
- Do not let decoration create cognitive effort.

---

## Design Translation

System 1 needs:

- strong first impression
- clear hierarchy
- emotionally relevant imagery
- visual credibility
- familiar navigation
- low clutter

System 2 needs:

- concrete information
- prices/details when relevant
- proof
- process
- FAQs
- clear next steps
- no hidden friction

Great websites satisfy both:

They feel right quickly, then hold up under scrutiny.

## AI Agent Instructions

When applying this book:

1. Audit the first 3 seconds of the page.
2. Remove competing visual priorities.
3. Make the visitor's next step obvious.
4. Check whether key claims have proof.
5. Use framing carefully and honestly.
6. Avoid relying only on aesthetics; support System 2 with substance.
7. Polish the ending: contact, checkout, reservation, confirmation, footer.

---

## Candidate Rules For Active Brain Later

- Users feel and scan before they reason.
- Premium design creates cognitive ease, not visual confusion.
- The hero anchors trust and perceived quality.
- Proof should appear near the moment of doubt.
- A site should feel good quickly and still make sense when inspected.
- Microinteractions are valuable when they create feedback, care, or a strong ending.
