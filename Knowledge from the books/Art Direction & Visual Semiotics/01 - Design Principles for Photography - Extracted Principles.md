# Design Principles for Photography - Extracted Principles

Source: Jeremy Webb, *Design Principles for Photography*  
Extraction quality: Usable. The PDF text extracted well enough to identify the main design principles. Some examples are visual, so this note focuses on transferable rules rather than trying to preserve the book's image-by-image analysis.

## Core Idea

Photography is not only about subject matter. It is a design act: framing, light, color, line, form, texture, contrast, focus, and empty space all decide how the viewer reads the image.

For websites, this means a photo should not be chosen only because it is attractive. It should have a clear visual job inside the section.

## Practical Principles

### 1. The Frame Is A Decision

A photo frame includes some information and excludes the rest. That exclusion is part of the message.

Website use:
- Crop hero images intentionally so the subject, brand signal, and text area all have room.
- Do not let automatic object-fit cropping hide the real product, sign, face, table, or atmosphere.
- If a photo does not survive the required crop, choose a different photo.

### 2. Composition Makes The Image Readable

The viewer should understand where to look first, second, and third.

Website use:
- A product photo needs a dominant subject.
- A place photo needs a clear spatial cue.
- A people photo needs a human expression, action, or relationship.
- Avoid busy images where no element is visually dominant.

### 3. Space Is A Design Tool

Empty space can make a subject feel calmer, more premium, or easier to read.

Website use:
- Use images with negative space when text must sit over them.
- Avoid placing headings on top of detailed texture, faces, or product details.
- Let image-heavy sections breathe; too many dense images make the brand feel noisy.

### 4. Light Changes Meaning

Lighting does more than reveal the subject. It controls mood, trust, and perceived quality.

Website use:
- Warm natural light often supports cafes, hospitality, craft, and local businesses.
- Harsh flash, dim blur, or mixed lighting can make a business feel careless.
- Dark cinematic lighting can work for fine dining, nightlife, luxury, or editorial brands, but can feel wrong for friendly daytime brands.

### 5. Texture Makes A Brand Feel Real

Texture communicates material truth: wood, fabric, glass, pastry crust, paper menus, stone, steam, skin, metal.

Website use:
- Use real textures from the business to avoid generic AI-smooth visuals.
- Close-up detail images can support atmosphere when they reveal something specific.
- Texture should not overpower clarity.

### 6. Contrast Guides Attention

Contrast can come from light/dark, color, sharp/soft focus, large/small scale, or stillness/movement.

Website use:
- Use contrast to make the CTA area obvious.
- Pair calm background photos with sharp foreground text.
- Avoid low-contrast hero sections where navigation or CTA text disappears.

### 7. Focus And Defocus Control Importance

Sharpness tells the viewer what matters. Blur tells the viewer what is secondary.

Website use:
- Product photos should keep the product sharp.
- Background lifestyle photos can use blur if they support atmosphere without becoming vague.
- Do not use blur to hide weak image quality; it still feels weak.

### 8. Pattern Can Build Identity

Repeated visual elements make a site feel coherent: cups, menu typography, tiles, table wood, flowers, signage, plates, branded napkins.

Website use:
- Identify recurring real-world brand motifs and repeat them through the site.
- Use patterns subtly. If every section repeats the same motif, the page becomes predictable.

## Website Checklist

- Does this image have one clear subject?
- Is the crop intentional for desktop and mobile?
- Does the image leave enough space for any text placed over it?
- Does the lighting match the desired brand mood?
- Does the photo reveal something real about the business?
- Does it help the user understand the product, place, people, or experience?
- Would the section become weaker if the image were removed?

## What To Avoid

- Choosing photos because they are simply pretty.
- Using decorative photos that do not prove anything.
- Letting automatic crops hide the useful part of the image.
- Using too many similar images in a row.
- Using generic atmosphere when the user needs to inspect the real business.

## Rule To Reuse Later

Every image must have a role: prove, orient, humanize, sell, explain, or create atmosphere. If it does none of those, remove it.
