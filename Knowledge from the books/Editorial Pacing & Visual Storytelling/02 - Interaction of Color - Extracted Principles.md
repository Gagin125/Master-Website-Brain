Source: Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color*

## Core Idea

Color does not exist in isolation. A color changes visually and emotionally depending on surrounding colors, scale, lightness, saturation, texture, and image context.

For websites, this means a color palette cannot be judged from swatches alone. It must be tested inside the actual page with real photos, real typography, and real section backgrounds.

## Key Principles

### 1. Color is relative

The same cream, green, brown, black, red, or gold can feel different depending on what surrounds it.

Website examples:

- cream can feel warm next to espresso brown
- cream can feel flat next to another similar cream section
- black can feel premium with warm photography
- black can feel too harsh for a soft local cafe
- gold can feel elegant in small amounts and cheap when overused

### 2. Contrast creates perception

Adjacent section colors shape how users perceive rhythm.

If two sections share nearly identical backgrounds, the page can feel flat.

If every section changes dramatically, the page can feel fragmented.

The best pacing uses controlled contrast.

### 3. A color's role matters

The same color can serve different roles:

- background
- text
- accent
- CTA
- border
- shadow
- image overlay
- active state

Color should have a job. Random color use weakens the brand.

### 4. Real assets should influence palette

A website with real photos should inspect those photos before finalizing the palette.

For hospitality:

- wood tones
- coffee tones
- pastry warmth
- plate colors
- interior lighting
- wall colors

should influence the final page.

### 5. Color contrast must support readability

Editorial color choices still need functional contrast.

Avoid:

- gray text over beige backgrounds with weak contrast
- white text over busy video without overlay
- gold text on cream if too low contrast
- colored links that feel unrelated to the palette

## Website Translation

### Background rhythm

Use color changes to create page pacing:

- light warm menu
- slightly different warm review section
- deeper gallery section
- structured contact section

Do not change backgrounds just because the design feels boring. First ask what the section should feel like.

### Gallery backgrounds

Dark gallery sections can work when images have strong warmth or contrast.

But for soft cafes, alternatives may work better:

- warm oat
- muted clay
- soft espresso tint
- desaturated sage
- deeper cream with a different temperature

### CTA colors

CTA color should be consistent and recognizable. It should not appear on non-clickable elements.

## Common AI Website Mistakes

- choosing a palette from swatches without testing photos
- using one background color for too many sections
- using dramatic black where a brand needs warmth
- using gold everywhere to signal premium
- relying on color alone for states
- ignoring contrast on mobile

## Candidate Rules For The Brain

- Color is contextual, not absolute.
- Test palette choices inside real layouts.
- Adjacent sections need controlled contrast.
- CTA color should be reserved for action.
- Real assets should influence color decisions.
- Premium color is usually disciplined color, not expensive-looking color.

## Rules Not To Overgeneralize

- Do not assume black always means luxury.
- Do not assume beige always means warmth.
- Do not assume green always means natural.
- Do not let color psychology override brand reality and asset reality.
