# Design Taste Rules

## Brand-Specific Design Rule

Every business needs its own website identity.

Do not clone one brand's layout into another brand.

Bad pattern:

- Use the same cafe layout for every cafe.
- Use the same luxury split layout for every premium business.
- Use the same menu/gallery/footer design across all projects.
- Assume "premium" means the same visual language everywhere.

Correct pattern:

- Study the business photos.
- Study the reviews.
- Study the location.
- Study the price level.
- Study the audience.
- Study the existing brand tone.
- Then design around that specific brand.

For one barber shop, premium may mean a moody lookbook and booking flow.

For another barber shop, premium may mean simple clarity, strong local proof, and fast calls.

For a plumber, premium usually means trust, speed, clarity, and emergency action, not luxury fashion layouts.

For a cafe, premium may mean warmth, real photos, menu clarity, and location ease.

The brand decides the design. The design system does not decide the brand.

## Book And Video Synthesis Rules

The books and videos should not be treated as equal commands.

Use them in this order:

1. Business truth.
2. User need.
3. Conversion goal.
4. Real proof and assets.
5. Book principles.
6. Modern video execution patterns.
7. Mobile, accessibility, speed, SEO, security, and launch reality.

### Active Rules

- Books explain why humans respond. Videos show how modern websites can express that response.
- Clarity beats cleverness at navigation, CTA, price/menu, booking, contact, and language switching.
- Atmosphere can be emotional, poetic, or mysterious, but actions must stay obvious.
- Direct response ideas are useful for offer, proof, specificity, and measurement, not for fake pressure.
- Use long information only when it is layered for scanners and serious buyers.
- Build a grid/type/color system first, then break it only when the break creates meaning.
- Color psychology is context, not formula. Real assets and category matter more than generic color meanings.
- Display typography creates mood. Reading typography creates trust.
- Motion must come from concept, usability, or feedback.
- Real photos are stronger than generic polish, but still need art direction.
- Systems prevent randomness. They should not erase brand personality.
- Psychology should reduce uncertainty and friction, not manipulate users.

### Do Not Promote These Into Design Decisions

- "Always use dark colors for premium."
- "Always use serif fonts for luxury."
- "Always use animation or 3D."
- "Always make the page minimal."
- "Always make the page persuasive with urgency."
- "Always break the grid."
- "Always copy award-site layouts."
- "Use color psychology as a fixed rule."
- "Use mystery to hide practical information."

## Dovydas's Emerging Design Taste

### What He Likes

- Real business photos over stock imagery.
- Real product/service visibility.
- Brand-specific colors and atmosphere.
- Clean hierarchy.
- Intentional whitespace.
- Editorial typography when the brand supports it.
- Clear, simple CTAs.
- Subtle motion that helps the user.
- Full-bleed or immersive sections when they serve the brand.
- Contact and location sections that feel structured and trustworthy.
- Footers that feel like part of the real business, not demo disclaimers.
- Mobile layouts that feel intentionally designed, not squeezed from desktop.
- Buttons that look obviously clickable.
- Microinteractions with purpose.
- Website sections that have one clear job.

### What He Dislikes

- Generic AI slop.
- Reused templates across brands.
- Centered generic blocks that feel like old landing page templates.
- Stock images when real photos exist.
- Decorative chips that look like buttons but do nothing.
- Huge 3D or complex animation with no conversion purpose.
- Redesigning unrelated sections during small edit requests.
- Copy changing when he only requested layout changes.
- Photos where the actual product is cropped badly.
- Mobile nav text squeezed into one row.
- Demo/disclaimer copy that makes a site feel unofficial when it is meant as a polished demo.
- Emojis in premium/luxury contact sections.
- Overexplaining inside the website UI.
- AI saying "yes" without evaluating whether the idea helps.

## Design Mistake Log

| Mistake | Why It Failed | Better Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Center-aligning large text blocks by default | It feels generic and weakens editorial structure | Use left-aligned text when the section is explanatory or premium/editorial |
| Making every section symmetrical | It looks templated and predictable | Use asymmetry when brand and content support it |
| Using stock-style photos | It makes the business feel less real | Prefer real photos, even imperfect ones, if they are clear and well-lit |
| Cropping images without checking the subject | Product/location gets hidden | Crop around the thing the user needs to inspect |
| Turning non-interactive labels into pill chips | Users read them as buttons and feel misled | Remove them or make them actually interactive |
| Copying previous design language | New brand feels fake or template-made | Start each brand with discovery |
| Overusing dark luxury aesthetics | Not every business should feel like a high-end restaurant | Match tone to business, price, audience, and goal |
| Oversized mobile menu links | The menu feels theatrical and impractical | Keep mobile menu expressive but compact enough to scan |
| Hiding language control at bottom of drawer | User cannot immediately find it | Put language switch close to page links |
| Contact sections with scattered links | The user has to hunt | Group address, hours, phone, email, social links logically |
| CTA duplication | Two buttons doing the same action create noise | One clear action is often stronger |
| Weak visual hierarchy in menu sections | Everything competes | Show preview first, then full detail on user action or separate page |

## Section-by-Section Thinking

### Navigation

Purpose:

- Help users move.
- Preserve brand identity.
- Keep CTA clear.

Risks:

- Too many links.
- Desktop nav squeezed into mobile.
- CTA duplication.
- Logo not visually dominant enough.

### Hero

Purpose:

- Answer what this is, who it is for, and why it matters.
- Create the first trust impression.

Risks:

- Video too slow.
- Text too narrow or too many lines.
- CTA hidden by visual noise.
- Headline too vague.

### About / Brand Story

Purpose:

- Build emotional trust.
- Explain the brand without resume-style self-talk.

Risks:

- Too much text.
- Overly complex words.
- Generic centered paragraphs.
- Photos that do not support the story.

### Menu / Services

Purpose:

- Show enough detail to build desire or decision confidence.

Risks:

- Too much upfront.
- No prices where prices matter.
- Preview and full menu/service list visually inconsistent.

### Gallery

Purpose:

- Prove the business is real.
- Show atmosphere and product quality.

Risks:

- Stock photos.
- Repeated photos.
- Crops hiding the subject.
- Uniform grid that feels generic.

### Contact / Visit

Purpose:

- Remove friction.
- Make location, hours, phone, booking, and maps obvious.

Risks:

- Scattered contact info.
- Buttons and social links visually fighting.
- Map too large or too small.
- Emojis in premium contexts.

### Footer

Purpose:

- Feel like a real business site.
- Provide final trust and navigation.

Risks:

- Demo disclaimers when not needed.
- Link farms.
- Weak brand ending.
